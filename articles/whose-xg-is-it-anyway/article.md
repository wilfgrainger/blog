# Whose xG Is It Anyway?

*What football recruitment can teach us about building AI agents that know what their data actually means.*

![Editorial illustration: an AI recruitment room evaluating Roberto McBaggio](images/01-hero.png)

There are easier ways to explain AI agents than football.

Football is better precisely because it is messy.

Ask an agent to find a striker and you quickly recreate half the problems involved in deploying AI inside a real organisation. Players share names and change clubs. Data comes from different providers. Metrics with the same label can mean different things. Some facts expire; some numbers are observations, others model outputs.

And then there is xG: assign each shot a probability, add them up, divide by minutes, rank the players. Reassuringly numerical. What could possibly go wrong?

Quite a lot. That is what makes football such a useful laboratory for agentic AI. Start with an LLM that answers questions; add club context, retrieval, tools and finally permission to act. Watch it become genuinely useful — then confidently select the wrong striker.

The interesting part is not that the AI made a mistake. Humans manage that every transfer window.

It is **why the system preferred the wrong player even though almost every individual step worked**.

An LLM can reason over information. A reliable agent also needs to know **what that information means**.

As decisions become more consequential, meaning cannot stay buried in prompts, documentation, SQL joins and experienced employees' heads. It has to become explicit enough for software to share, inspect and challenge.

That is where an ontology starts to matter: not because every AI system needs one, but because repeatedly confusing one thing for another can eventually cost more than formalising what those things mean.

To see why, we need a fictional striker called Roberto McBaggio.

---

## 10:06 on Monday morning

At 10:06, the club's new AI recruitment assistant finds its next number nine.

The brief is simple:

> Find under-23 forwards in Europe with at least 900 league minutes and more than 0.55 non-penalty expected goals per 90. Exclude players who would obviously be outside our budget.

Thirty seconds later, Roberto McBaggio appears.

Twenty-one years old. Central Europe. Eighteen months left on his contract. He presses, runs the channels, and the assistant has even found six clips of him arriving between the centre-back and full-back.

And then there is the number: **0.64 xG per 90.**

For approximately nine glorious minutes, recruitment has been solved.

Then Mark, one of the analysts, asks two irritatingly short questions.

> “Whose xG?”

Nobody answers.

> “And did it remove the penalties?”

It did not.

In our fictional dataset, McBaggio's 0.64 came from 7.27 xG over 1,020 minutes. But 2.37 of that was generated by three penalties, each valued at 0.79 by the provider used for those records. Remove them and the arithmetic becomes `(7.27 - 2.37) / 1,020 × 90 = 0.43` non-penalty xG per 90.

He no longer meets the brief.

There is another problem. The assistant has ranked players across leagues using xG supplied by two different providers. Both columns are called `xG`, but the values came from different models, with different features and assumptions.

Those are two separate failures. **The penalty mistake makes McBaggio a false positive. The provider mismatch means parts of the shortlist should never have been ranked against each other in the first place.**

Every database row is real. The arithmetic is correct. The tools all returned successfully. The answer is beautifully formatted.

The shortlist is wrong.

McBaggio may be brilliant, and Mark may still want to scout him. But he is a **red herring for this particular question**.

The agent has not found the player who best satisfies the brief. It has found the player who looks best after subtly different meanings were flattened into the same tidy columns.

---

## Stage one: give the model a job it cannot break

Two weeks earlier, the assistant had a safer job: explain why a team can produce more xG from fewer shots, summarise the opposition's build-up patterns, and translate “rest defence” into language a board member can repeat without frightening the coaching staff.

It was excellent. That is close to the environment in which large language models are most comfortable: take language and context, reason over them, and produce more language.

The important word is **produce**. A chatbot can be wrong and produce a bad paragraph. An agent can be wrong and **change something**.

That gap matters more as AI systems move from short exchanges towards delegated, long-running work. OpenAI's June 2026 analysis describes agents operating for minutes or hours, orchestrating tools and iterating towards outcomes rather than simply returning one answer. [OpenAI, *How agents are transforming work*, June 2026](https://openai.com/index/how-agents-are-transforming-work/).

Football lets us watch that transition happen: our assistant starts in the press box, then gets the keys to the scouting department.

---

## First, give it the club handbook

The model knows football.

It does not know **our club's football**.

It knows roughly what xG means, but not which provider the club approves; what “league” means, but not whether play-offs count; what a contract is, but not which feed is authoritative.

So we begin with the least glamorous architecture in this article: Markdown.

A few files:

- `RECRUITMENT_POLICY.md`
- `APPROVED_DATA_SOURCES.md`
- `METRIC_DEFINITIONS.md`
- `WHEN_TO_ASK_MARK.md`

For many tasks, that may be enough. The files are cheap, versionable and human-readable; more importantly, they expose where the ambiguity lives before anybody commissions a twelve-month “Football Knowledge Fabric” programme and orders the branded quarter-zips.

Now add retrieval. When the assistant needs the definition of non-penalty xG or the club's competition rules, the relevant material can be brought into context. It is an open-book exam with an extremely fast librarian.

But librarians solve an **availability problem**, not automatically a **meaning problem**. The assistant can retrieve the correct definition and still apply it to the wrong dataset; retrieve two providers' methodology notes and still treat their outputs as interchangeable; retrieve yesterday's contract perfectly when today's record supersedes it.

**Retrieval gets information into the room. It does not make everything in the room compatible.**

---

## Before graphs, there is a useful middle

This is where architecture conversations can get silly.

The choice is not:

**Markdown or enormous enterprise ontology.**

There is a lot of useful engineering in between.

A recruitment system can introduce typed player identifiers, a metric registry that distinguishes total xG from non-penalty xG, data contracts that require provider and model version, foreign keys tying contracts to players, and validation that rejects incompatible providers.

A Pydantic model, relational schema and a few ruthless checks may solve an entire class of failures without a graph database anywhere in sight. That is good architecture. The purpose is not to graduate towards a graph because graphs look sophisticated. It is to make **meaning harder to lose at system boundaries**.

> Markdown → structured schemas → typed contracts → validated joins → shared semantics → graph where relationships genuinely justify one

![From information to meaning: RAG, tools, schemas, semantic layer, deterministic controls, evidence-backed answer](images/03-semantic-stack.png)

Most teams should climb only as high as the problem requires. The practical trigger is this: **you probably do not need an ontology because you have an LLM. You start needing one when the same consequential concepts are interpreted repeatedly across multiple datasets, systems, teams or agents — and different interpretations produce different decisions.**

That is what happens as our football assistant acquires more tools, data sources and authority. Semantics becomes a shared architectural concern.

---

## Then the chatbot gets a data subscription

The team gives the assistant tools.

Player search. Match and event data. Contract records. Video. A calculator. Perhaps the ability to write a shortlist back into the recruitment system.

Now the assistant can do something more interesting than discuss football. It can decide: *I need Roberto McBaggio's league appearances. Now his shots. Now their expected-goals values. Now his minutes. Now I can calculate the answer.*

This is where the architecture becomes agentic in the practical sense: the model is inside a harness, choosing actions, receiving results and adapting. Anthropic's January 2026 work distinguishes the model from that wider **agent harness** — tools, environment, state, instructions and the mechanisms through which it affects the world — and argues that the transcript is not enough; what matters is the resulting state of the environment. [Anthropic, *Demystifying evals for AI agents*, January 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents).

That is precisely our recruitment problem.

The assistant can *say* it followed the brief.

We need to know whether the shortlist actually did.

## Five green ticks. Wrong striker.

![Five green ticks ending in the wrong striker](images/02-five-green-ticks.png)

Here is the annoying part.

The player lookup succeeds.

The appearance query succeeds.

The event query succeeds.

The arithmetic succeeds.

The shortlist write succeeds.

Five green ticks.

Wrong striker.

This is a more useful way to think about many agent failures than simply saying “the LLM hallucinated”.

The model may not have invented anything.

The failure can be in the **composition**.

Select the wrong competition and every later calculation can be flawless.

Merge two players with similar names and the radar chart can look magnificent for a footballer who exists only in SQL.

Treat two providers' xG as interchangeable and the system can rank incomparable measurements to three decimal places.

It is the football-data equivalent of putting the wrong stadium into the sat-nav.

Every turn can be correct.

You are still missing kick-off.

Anthropic's April 2026 managed-agent architecture makes the same distinction by separating the **brain**, the **hands** and the **session** so reasoning, execution and state can be controlled independently. [Anthropic, *Scaling Managed Agents: Decoupling the brain from the hands*, April 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/managed-agents).

For football the language is less dramatic, but the principle is the same: the part deciding what to do does not have to be the part trusted to decide what is true.

---

## xG is not a fact found growing on the pitch

This is why xG is such a useful semantic trap.

Expected goals is not like a player's date of birth.

It is a **model output**.

A July 2026 paper in *Intelligent Sports and Health* describes xG as a probabilistic estimate based on features such as shot location, angle, body part and match context, and warns against treating it as a deterministic account of what “should” have happened. Model design and context affect interpretation. [Lago-Peñas et al., *A probabilistic and dynamic reformulation of expected goals (xG): Methodological advances and modelling perspectives*, July 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ish.2026.05.001).

That gives us a useful problem.

A database cell may contain `0.64`, but that is not enough. To use it properly, the system may need the metric, provider, model version, population of shots, competition scope, time period, penalty treatment, minutes definition and aggregation rule.

The database contains a number. **The semantic layer tells us what kind of number it is.**

That is what the tidy column heading hides. `xG` looks like height or age: one thing measured repeatedly. It is closer to a recipe. Two dishes can have the same name and still not contain the same ingredients.

Football analysts know this instinctively because they spend their lives asking what a metric includes, what it excludes and which strange behaviour it accidentally rewards.

Much of that knowledge is documented.

An uncomfortable amount lives in Mark.

---

## Mark is the club's latent ontology

Mark does not merely “know the data”.

He knows that these two Roberto McBaggio records refer to different people. That “league appearances” means this set of competitions. That Provider A and Provider B are not comparable for this screen. That this column includes penalties, this contract record supersedes the other one, and when *that* number looks suspicious, you check *this* feed.

Mark is, in effect, an undocumented API with an annual-leave allowance.

More importantly, he contains part of the club's **latent ontology**.

Before the club formalises an ontology, one already exists. It lives in people, SQL, spreadsheets, code, documentation, Slack threads and, occasionally, the sentence:

> “Oh yeah. Don't use that column.”

Organisations do not usually lack meaning. They lack **explicit, shared, machine-checkable meaning**.

The agent has started crossing joins Mark used to cross in his head: which player, which competition, which shots, which provider, which model version, which minutes, which fact is current, and what happens when two authoritative sources disagree? Another thousand pages in the prompt will not settle all of those questions.

At some point, repeatedly consequential ambiguity deserves structure.

---

## Now the ontology earns its entrance

This is where an ontology starts doing real work: not because it sounds more serious than a schema, but because the system now needs a **shared semantic contract** across data, tools, agents and humans.

Three things are worth separating. The **ontology** defines the agreed concepts and relationships. The **semantic layer** maps and exposes those meanings consistently across systems. A **knowledge graph** is one useful way to store and traverse actual relationships when the domain needs it.

Good data engineering can enforce meaning inside a system boundary. An ontology becomes valuable when the same meaning has to survive across many boundaries — datasets, services, teams and agents — without each one inventing its own interpretation.

None of that replaces the engineering underneath. The ontology does not deduplicate a player or remove a penalty from a calculation. It establishes what a `Player`, `Metric`, `Provider`, `ModelVersion`, `Appearance` and `Competition` mean, and which relationships matter. Identifiers, data-quality rules, deterministic services and validation code do the mechanical work.

**Ontology supplies the semantic contract. It does not perform all the work.**

In our football example, the contract can say that a player makes an appearance in a match, a match belongs to a competition, a shot belongs to an appearance, and an xG observation was produced by a particular provider and model version. A graph can then hold the actual instances of those relationships.

The route to the number becomes explicit:

> player → appearances → matches → competition → shots → penalty status → xG model → aggregate → minutes

I think of this as the **evidence path**. The answer can now be reconstructed rather than merely accepted:

> McBaggio recorded 0.43 non-penalty xG per 90 across 1,020 qualifying league minutes. The 0.64 total included three penalties. All included shot values came from the same approved provider and model version. He does not meet the 0.55 recruitment threshold.

Now rerun the original search. “Under 23”, “league minutes” and “non-penalty expected goals” resolve against the club's agreed definitions. The calculation service enforces the penalty rule. Provider and model-version compatibility is checked before ranking. Roberto McBaggio drops out of first place because he never met the stated threshold.

The provider problem is handled separately. **If only some candidates lack comparable evidence, withhold those candidates and rank the valid remainder. If the comparability problem undermines the whole cohort, refuse the ranking and explain what is missing.** What the system must never do is silently mix incompatible evidence and call the result comparable.

Another player — previously buried beneath the false positive — rises to the top of the valid shortlist. That player may not be the objectively “best footballer”; no ontology can settle that. But the system has finally found the player who best satisfies **the question the club actually asked**.

Without the semantic layer, the agent found the player who looked best in the available data. With it, the surrounding system can preserve the meaning of the brief, enforce the rules and reject invalid comparisons.

The ontology has not made the model smarter.

It has removed a red herring.

## Ontology is not a truth machine

There is an important limit here.

A beautifully modelled ontology can still encode something wrong.

A knowledge graph is a map, and maps can be beautifully drawn while still sending the team bus towards a bridge that closed last week.

Perhaps McBaggio changed clubs yesterday, two providers disagree about whether an event is a shot, the provider changed its model halfway through the dataset, or somebody linked the wrong Roberto McBaggio.

The relationships therefore need the boring things architecture diagrams tend to put in tiny boxes near the bottom: **sources, ownership, effective dates, confidence, approval and conflict resolution.** Facts extracted by an LLM can be proposed for review; they should not become approved truth simply because the extraction arrived wearing JSON.

Recent work on dynamically generated ontologies needs the same caution. An August 2026 preprint, **OaK**, explores constructing task-oriented ontologies and knowledge graphs for LLM agents and reports improved grounding across several benchmarks. [Zhang et al., *Toward Effective and Reliable LLM Agents via Dynamic Ontology*, August 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22974).

**Runtime semantic discovery is not the same thing as institutional truth.** An agent may propose that two concepts are related; it should not be able to redefine an approved metric, legal relationship or risk rule because doing so makes the task easier. Dynamic construction can discover candidate structure. Governed definitions still need authoritative sources and ownership where the consequences demand it.

---

## Some rules should never be semantic debates

The ontology tells the system what “non-penalty xG” means.

That does not mean the LLM should personally remember to remove penalties every time.

If the recruitment brief requires non-penalty xG from one approved model, a deterministic query or validation service should enforce the condition.

If the data cannot satisfy it, the request should fail closed.

The model should not be allowed to replace a failed validation with a stirring paragraph about movement in the box.

This gives us one of the simplest rules in the whole architecture:

**Do not ask a probabilistic component to enforce a deterministic invariant if ordinary software can enforce it instead.**

The ontology makes the invariant semantically clear.

Code makes it mechanically unavoidable.

Those are different jobs.

Reliable agent architecture is largely about deciding which job belongs where.

---

## Every task has an ambiguity budget

There is still plenty we do not want to formalise away.

Football recruitment is partly judgement.

Whether McBaggio will adapt to another league is not a database fact waiting to be retrieved.

Whether his movement against a low block compensates for weaker numbers is not something the ontology should decide.

A scout is supposed to form hypotheses.

That gives us an **ambiguity budget**: the amount of unresolved interpretation a workflow can tolerate before it needs more evidence, human judgement or a stop.

The club can tolerate uncertainty about whether McBaggio will become a better player next season. It should not tolerate uncertainty about whether a metric labelled “non-penalty” still contains three penalties.

Those are different kinds of uncertainty: one belongs to judgement; the other is an avoidable semantic defect.

## And every agent sits somewhere on an authority gradient

There is a second dimension: authority accumulates.

**Explain** — “What does xG mean?”  
**Recommend** — “Which strikers should we watch?”  
**Decide** — “Build the final recruitment shortlist.”  
**Act** — “Contact the agents of the top five.”  
**Commit** — “Submit a £25 million offer.”

The underlying model may be identical.

The authority is not.

As the agent moves down that gradient, evidence, permissions, deterministic checks, recovery and approvals matter more. Anthropic's May 2026 engineering write-up on agent containment makes the same point from a security angle: greater capability and access increase potential blast radius, so hard environmental and permission boundaries matter alongside probabilistic safeguards. [Anthropic, *How we contain Claude across products*, May 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/how-we-contain-claude).

So our architecture has two useful questions.

**Ambiguity budget:** how uncertain may the system be?

**Authority gradient:** how much may it do about its answer?

The more authority we give an agent, the less comfortably we can leave consequential meaning implicit.

That is where ontology becomes part of the control system rather than an academic exercise.

## 14:17, same architecture, different consequences

At 14:17, the architecture gets another job.

An asset-management agent is asked whether two issuers belong to the same corporate group before a trade is approved.

It finds the entities, an ownership record and the trading mandate. The relationship check returns green. The limit calculation returns green. The proposed trade is inside the numerical threshold.

Five green ticks.

There is just one problem.

The ownership relationship came from yesterday's source.

A newer filing shows that the intermediate holding company changed this morning.

The names are real.

The entities are real.

The calculation is correct.

The relationship is stale.

We have met Roberto McBaggio again.

Only the nouns have changed.

## Finance has the same semantic problem

![Football concepts mapped to analogous financial concepts](images/05-football-to-finance.png)

Replace **Player** with **Issuer**, **Club** with **Legal Entity**, **Registration** with **Ownership Relationship**, **xG Model** with **Valuation Methodology**, and **Recruitment Rule** with **Trading Mandate**. Then ask the same questions: which entity, according to which source, effective when, through which relationship, was it superseded, can the answer be reconstructed, and what happens when authoritative sources disagree?

The football has disappeared. The architecture has not.

Financial services is full of terms that look simple until software has to act on them: issuer, obligor, guarantor, beneficial owner, counterparty, portfolio, exposure, mandate, instrument, legal entity. Each sits inside relationships, roles, time and jurisdiction.

The [Financial Industry Business Ontology, FIBO](https://edmcouncil.org/financial-industry-business-ontology/) exists to define financial concepts and relationships in machine-readable form; in August 2026, the EDM Association even ran [*Why Your AI Needs an Ontology: Getting Started with FIBO*](https://edmconnect.edmcouncil.org/events/event-description?CalendarEventKey=4ed40715-05bb-441d-b6d0-01a001f902d3&Home=%2Fevents%2Fcalendar). The point is not to import FIBO wholesale. It is that **if several agents, systems and humans make consequential decisions using the same concepts, they need a shared way of saying what those concepts mean.**

The semantics also sit inside a wider control architecture. In its July 2026 Financial Stability Report, the Bank of England discusses more autonomous AI systems in finance and **Project Logos**, which aims to let central banks observe and analyse LLM-based agents acting as portfolio managers in a simulated financial market. [Bank of England, *Financial Stability Report*, July 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/financial-stability-report/2026/july-2026).

Minutes of the Bank and FCA AI Consortium's **3 June 2026 meeting, published on 5 August**, go closer to the engineering detail: members discussed **model harnesses** and **execution boundaries** separating probabilistic LLM reasoning from deterministic system actions. [Bank of England, *Artificial Intelligence Consortium minutes – June 2026*, published August 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/minutes/2026/june/ai-consortium-minutes-3-june-2026).

The football example adds the semantic point. An execution boundary can stop an agent exceeding a trading limit; it cannot rescue a limit calculation built on the wrong issuer relationship. Permissions can stop an agent writing to a system; they cannot make incompatible metrics comparable. A deterministic validator can enforce an approved rule, but it still needs a stable definition of the concepts in that rule.

That is why semantics and controls belong together.

---

## RAG gives the agent information. Ontology gives the information meaning.

By this point the layers have different jobs. RAG gets relevant material into context. Tools let the model query, calculate and act. Schemas give interfaces structure. Deterministic services enforce rules that should not be probabilistic. Ontologies become useful when **meaning itself must remain consistent across those boundaries**.

They answer questions such as: *What is this thing? How is it related to that thing? Which definition applies here? When is that relationship valid? What evidence makes the claim acceptable?*

A conversational assistant can survive a fuzzy definition and ask a follow-up question. A system approving a trade, moving money or changing a customer outcome has much less room for semantic drift.

**The more authority we give agents, the less we can afford to leave meaning implicit.**

---

## Do not start by building the ontology

That sentence needs an immediate warning label.

If the first outcome of this article is a twelve-month enterprise-ontology programme, I have explained it badly. Start with one narrow workflow: good instructions and context, retrieval, typed schemas and data contracts, the minimum useful tools, and deterministic checks around rules that must always hold.

Then watch where the experts keep correcting it. Collect the awkward cases: duplicate entities, incompatible metrics, superseded policies, temporal relationships, conflicting sources and all the things “everybody knows” that have never actually been written down.

Measure whether decisions are supported and reconstructable, whether the wrong candidate survives because two meanings were treated as one, and whether the same human keeps repairing the same ambiguity. Then formalise the concepts and relationships that repeatedly cause consequential mistakes.

Use an ontology where shared meaning matters. Use a graph where relationship traversal matters. Use schemas where structure is enough. Use code where the rule is deterministic. Use retrieval where the problem is access to context. Use people where judgement is the actual job.

That is not a maturity model.

It is a toolbox.

---

## Back to Monday morning

The recruitment assistant runs the query again.

This time Roberto McBaggio does not sit at the top of the shortlist.

“Non-penalty xG” has an explicit definition and the calculation service enforces it. Incompatible provider data is rejected. “League” resolves to the competitions the club actually means. Player and contract identities resolve through the right relationships. The evidence path can be reconstructed.

Another player rises above McBaggio.

That player was always in the data. The first system simply could not see past the red herring it had created for itself.

Nothing about the LLM became more intelligent. We made the meaning around it more explicit.

The LLM can reason. Truth can live in databases, meaning in an ontology, relationships in a graph, structure in schemas, calculations in code, and permissions outside the model entirely.

The agent's job is to navigate those things intelligently without quietly changing what they mean.

And one of its most valuable answers will occasionally be:

> I can't establish that from comparable evidence yet.

Mark looks at the revised shortlist.

McBaggio is no longer first.

He puts him back on the scouting list anyway because he likes his movement against a low block.

And that is fine.

Human judgement was never the bug.

The bug was allowing a machine to present incompatible meanings as one comparable fact.

Football just made it easier to see.

And Mark can finally go on holiday without taking the club's ontology with him.
